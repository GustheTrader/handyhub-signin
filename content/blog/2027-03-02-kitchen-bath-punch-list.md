A kitchen or bath is a set of systems stacked in a small wet footprint: water supply, drain, heat, ventilation, and cabinetry. None of them fail loudly. Here is what to check, in the order that costs least to fix.

## The water heater, first, because it is the worst failure

- **T&P valve** (temperature and pressure relief): pull the lever once a year, a quart of water should flow and stop. If it weeps continuously or will not reseat, replace it. A stuck T&P valve is how a water heater becomes a projectile.
- **The anode rod** is what actually protects the tank. It sacrifices itself, and when it is gone, the tank rusts from the inside. Replacing an anode rod at year six is a $150–$250 service that extends tank life by years. Most tanks are never serviced.
- **Flush the tank** once a year to remove sediment. Sediment on the bottom is why a tank rumbles and why it runs out of hot water faster than its rating.
- **Age:** the first four digits of the serial number are the date code. Most tanks are 8–12 year units. A 14-year-old tank has already beaten the odds; a drain pan and a shut-off are the minimum insurance.
- **The drain pan** with a pipe to a drain is the single best $80 in this list.

## Under the sink: the highest-risk cabinet in the house

- **The P-trap** should be the only trap. A double trap, or an S-trap, siphons and smells.
- **The supply valves** should close. Test them, do not assume. A valve that will not close means the main gets shut off for every small repair.
- **The cabinet floor** is the indicator. Water staining and a swollen bottom board mean a slow leak that has been going for months.
- **The disposal** connection is the second most common leak, and the flange gasket hardens. A disposal that hums and does not spin is jammed, not dead; the hex key in the bottom is the first step.
- **A dishwasher supply line** older than ten years is a braided stainless swap. The failure mode is a burst line under a cabinet while nobody is home.

## The faucet

- **A drip from the spout** is a cartridge or a seat, $10–$40 for the part. Replacing the whole faucet because of a cartridge is a common and expensive mistake.
- **A leak at the base** is an O-ring. Note whether the faucet is single-handle or two, and the brand; cartridges are model-specific.
- **A faucet that is loose** is a setting nut under the sink. Access is the whole job.
- **A pull-down sprayer that drips** is a diverter.
- **Ask for the model number** before we start. A photo of the faucet and one of the underside saves a second visit.

## The drain and the smell

A drain smell is almost always the trap, the vent, or the flange seal.

- **The trap should hold water.** A trap in a bathroom used seasonally evaporates, and the sewer gas comes up. Pour a quart of water down it.
- **The toilet's wax ring** seals the flange. A rocking toilet breaks that seal, and the smell is the symptom long before water appears.
- **A blocked vent stack** (nest, leaf, or ice) makes a gurgle and a smell. A bathroom that gurgles when the toilet flushes has a vent problem, not a drain problem.
- **A floor drain in a laundry room** with no trap primer dries out. A few tablespoons of mineral oil on the water surface slows evaporation.

## Tile, grout, and the shower that will not dry

- **Lippage**, one tile edge higher than its neighbour, is an installation defect beyond about 1/32 inch. It is not a grout fix.
- **A soft or hollow tile** sounds different when tapped. A hollow tile over a wet substrate means the substrate is compromised.
- **Grout in a shower** needs sealing yearly, and a squeegee after every use does more than any sealer.
- **A shower door that will not close** is a pivot or a hinge alignment, not a new shower.
- **The silicone at the tub-to-wall joint** is a wear item. Replace it when it discolours, not when it fails.

## Cabinetry and the hardware that carries the load

- **A drawer that will not close squarely** is a slide out of alignment or a loose screw. A drawer slide has an adjustment on the front and back; a stripped screw hole gets a dowel and glue, not a bigger screw.
- **Soft-close hinges** are adjustable in two or three axes with a screwdriver. A door that hits the frame next door is a five-minute hinge adjustment.
- **Upper cabinets** must be anchored into studs. A cabinet full of plates is heavy, and toggle bolts into drywall alone are not enough.
- **A cabinet that has pulled away from the wall** at the top is a fastener failure and a hazard.
- **Under-cabinet lighting** and the wiring behind it: nothing but a proper fixture and a proper connection.

## Kitchen and bath ventilation

- **Range hood:** a hood that recirculates filters grease but does not remove moisture or combustion products. If there is a gas range, it must vent outside.
- **Bath fan:** 1 CFM per square foot, minimum 50 CFM, ducted outside and insulated in the attic.
- **Dryer vent** — if the laundry is in the bath or kitchen, the dryer duct must be clean, rigid where possible, and short. A clogged dryer vent is a fire path.

## The assembly jobs that get skipped

- **Cabinet and drawer hardware** installed with a template costs less than re-drilling a door you already drilled.
- **A new dishwasher** needs the supply, drain, and power checked before the old one leaves. Half the "new dishwasher is broken" calls are an air gap or a drain hose loop.
- **A vanity install** is mostly the water connections and the level. A vanity that is out of level shows at the door reveal, not the counter.

## The punch list, in order

1. **Test the T&P valve** and note the water heater's date code.
2. **Turn every under-sink shut-off** by hand.
3. **Look at the cabinet floor** for staining and swelling.
4. **Pour a quart of water down any drain that is rarely used.**
5. **Tighten and adjust** every hinge and drawer slide.
6. **Seal the grout** in the shower once a year.
