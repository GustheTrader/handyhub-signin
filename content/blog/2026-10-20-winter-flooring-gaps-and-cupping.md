If your hardwood opened up thin dark lines along every seam this winter, nothing is wrong with your house. Wood is a sponge that was cut to fit in a specific moisture state, and Reno/Sparks drives that state harder than almost anywhere in the country.

We are at roughly 4,500 feet with a high desert climate: single-digit indoor humidity is normal in January, and monsoon-season humidity in July is a different world. A floor installed in June and inspected in January has lived through two completely different houses.

## What is actually happening

Wood floorboards expand across their width as they absorb moisture and shrink as they dry. Nothing you can seal onto the surface changes that; even a factory finish only slows the movement at the edges.

Three symptoms, three different causes:

- **Gaps between boards, no change in board flatness.** Seasonal shrinkage. Expected, and reversible.
- **Edges curling up like a shallow bowl.** Cupping. Usually moisture from underneath, meaning a leak, a slab, or a crawlspace.
- **Middle of the board high, edges down.** Crowning. This is the one that usually follows a bad cupping repair, where someone sanded a cupped floor flat before fixing the moisture.

Cupping and crowning are not cosmetic. Gaps in a dry winter usually close on their own by August.

## The numbers that matter

If you want to make a flooring decision with data rather than feel:

- Keep indoor **relative humidity between 35% and 55%**. Below 30% is where gapping accelerates, and above 60% is where mold and cupping risk starts.
- For solid hardwood, target **6% to 9% wood moisture content** in this climate, measured with a pin meter across several boards. Installers should log it; ask for it if you are having new floor put in.
- For **LVP and laminate**, the floor itself does not absorb water, but it does move thermally. Manufacturers typically specify a **1/4-inch expansion gap** at every wall and a **transition at runs longer than 20 to 30 feet**. A plank pushed tight to a door jamb in June will tent in the middle of the room in August.

That last one is the most common failure we are called to fix, and it is a thirty-minute repair rather than a floor replacement: pull the baseboard, cut the plank back, reinstall.

## What to do about gaps, in order of cost

1. **Nothing, yet.** If the gaps are under about 1/8 inch and the boards are flat, wait for spring. A humidifier running at 40% will close most of them within a month.
2. **Add humidity.** A whole-house humidifier on the furnace is $450–$900 installed and is the actual fix. It also stops the cracking wood door trim, the squeaky stair, and the drywall tape lifting at the ceiling corners that show up in the same season.
3. **Fill the gaps.** Wood filler colored to match is $150–$400 for a room and looks convincing for a season or two. It does not stop movement, and it cracks again. Be honest with yourself before paying for it.
4. **Refinish or replace.** Only warranted if boards are cupped or crowned, or the gaps exceed 3/16 inch and the humidity has already been corrected for a full year.

## The door test you can run tonight

A door that sticks in July and swings free in January is telling you the same story. Floor and door frame move together in the dry months, and the two bind in the humid months. If you have a door that rubs from mid-June to September, it needs the top edge planed or the hinge side adjusted, not a new slab. We do this in a single visit with the strip taken off the top edge and the door rehung, and it costs a fraction of a replacement.

## What to ask any installer

- "What is the moisture content of my subfloor and my flooring, and will you write it down?"
- "How far from the wall are you leaving the expansion gap, and where are your transitions?"
- "If this gaps next winter, what does your warranty actually cover?"
