Every bathroom fan in the Truckee Meadows has two jobs: pull moisture out of the room, and pull it out of the wall. Most of the ones we open up fail the second job, and the homeowner finds out two winters later through a paint bubble on the ceiling below.

This is the single cheapest preventive repair on a house, and it is almost always cheaper than the drywall it prevents.

## The code number most local fans miss

Nevada adopts the International Residential Code, and IRC **M1507.3 and M1507.4** set the bar for bathroom exhaust. The room needs intermittent mechanical exhaust of **50 CFM** with a manual switch that is either a **20-minute or longer timer** or a **humidistat**. You keep the **5-year warranty** honest on our work, but a fan installed over a builder-grade 12-minute turn-off does not meet the intent, because nobody is going to stand there and count.

Two more things the code is specific about, because they are where moisture escapes:

- **Duct termination.** M1507.3.3 requires the exhaust to terminate **outside** the building envelope, fitted with a backdraft damper.
- **Vent termination in attics is not a pass.** IRC M1506.2 prohibits terminating exhaust in an attic or crawlspace.

If you have ever been in your attic in January, you may have seen the short flex duct draped across the insulation or laid over the top plates. That is a fan moving the problem, not fixing it. In our climate, dumped attic moisture condenses on the roof sheathing, and the visible result is the dark staining around nails and on the sheathing underside.

## Test yours in 30 seconds

Turn the fan on and let it run two minutes, then hold a single square of toilet paper flat against the grille.

- **Holds firm** at all four corners: the fan is pulling.
- **Flaps or falls**: airflow is blocked, the motor is tired, or the duct has come off the boot upstairs.

If the paper holds, walk outside while it is running and stand near the vent on the roof or wall. You should feel air. If you do not, the duct has likely popped off at the roof cap, or the line is pinched. Drywall dust in the grille and insect screen fouling the wheel are the two most common causes of a fan that is "on" but doing nothing.

## The three outcomes, honestly priced

| Finding | What it means | Ballpark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dust and lint on the grille and wheel | Clean, reattach | $95–$150 |
| Motor is louder than the air | Replace motor or whole fan | $225–$400 delivered |
| Fan works, 15-minute builder switch | Timer or humidistat added | $75–$150 |
| Flex duct loose, torn, or in the attic | Reroute or replace in rigid, vent to exterior | $250–$650, attic access dependent |

A warning on the last row. If your attic has blown-in insulation, plan on a second visit to clean up after the line is replaced, because the insulation you displace has to be put back. Any quote that does not mention this is a quote that is going to surprise you.

## Why the timer matters more than you think

Between November and February, Reno/Sparks houses sit in two extremes: outdoor air below freezing, indoor air heated dry. That means the humid air from a 20-minute shower is not going anywhere on its own. A fan that stops at twelve minutes leaves the moisture in the room, on the mirror, in the grout, and in the drywall glue.

Set the timer to 20 or 25 minutes and you will not think about it again. Set it to 12 and you will replace the same fan in five years with a much more expensive problem attached.
