Eighty percent of residential electrical fires involve an arc or a loose connection, not a short. That is why the fixes below are mostly about stopping things from coming loose and getting warm.

The ones a homeowner can do are marked **DIY**. The ones that need a licensed electrician are marked **LICENSED**. Please respect the difference: the licensed items are the ones where the mistake is invisible until it is a fire.

## 1. Every GFCI receptacle in the house, tested (DIY)

Press **TEST**, listen for the click, press **RESET**. Every kitchen counter, bathroom, garage, laundry, and exterior receptacle should be GFCI or on a GFCI breaker. If the test button does not click, the device is dead and is no longer protecting you.

**One GFCI can protect the downstream receptacles on the same circuit.** If a bathroom receptacle will not reset, find the GFCI in the other bathroom, the garage, or the panel, because a tripped upstream device kills the whole run.

**In winter,** a GFCI that trips repeatedly on an exterior receptacle is usually moisture in the box, not a failed device. That is a licensed fix.

## 2. Replace the wallpaper of cheap power strips (DIY)

A $12 strip rated at 15 amps is fine for a lamp and a phone charger. It is not fine for a space heater, a hair dryer, or a microwave.

**The number to read:** amps on the device label against amps on the strip. A 1,500-watt space heater draws **12.5 amps at 120 volts**, which is 83% of a 15-amp circuit, on its own. Add a TV and you are at the limit.

**Never daisy-chain strips.** Stacked strips multiply loose connections and heat, and a strip plugged into a strip is the exact configuration in most strip-related fire reports.

## 3. Space heater rules that prevent the breaker trip (DIY)

A single 1,500-watt heater on a dedicated 20-amp circuit is comfortable. Two on one circuit is 25 amps on a 20-amp breaker, and the trip is the breaker doing its job.

- **One heater per circuit.**
- **The heater gets its own receptacle,** not a strip.
- **Three feet of clearance** from anything combustible, and never under a desk with papers.
- **An oil-filled radiator** draws the same 1,500 watts as a ceramic box, but it cycles.
- **A 240-volt heater** (a wall unit) is 2–3 times more efficient at heating the same room than a plug-in, and it is a licensed install.

**Breakers that trip under load** are protecting you. A breaker that is warm to the touch, or that smells, is a licensed call today.

## 4. Loose cover plates and a missing box extension (DIY)

A receptacle in drywall with no box, or a box set back more than 1/4 inch behind the finished surface, is a code violation and a fire path: the arc happens outside the box, in the wall.

**Check behind every switch and receptacle you can reach:** the plate should sit flat, the device should not move when a plug is inserted, and the box should be flush with the drywall or have a listed extension ring.

**A plug that falls out of the receptacle** is worn contacts, and the device should be replaced by a licensed electrician if you are not comfortable working live. Turn the breaker off, verify with a tester, and replace.

## 5. The smoke and CO combination (DIY)

- **Smoke alarms:** one per bedroom, one outside each sleeping area, one per level, and one in a basement.
- **Replace the whole unit every 10 years,** not just the battery. The sensor degrades. The manufacture date is on the back.
- **A combination CO alarm** near the furnace and the water heater is not a duplicate, it is the one that catches a cracked heat exchanger.
- **Photoelectric** is better for smouldering fires, **ionisation** for flaming. A combination unit is the compromise.

**Do not paint a smoke alarm.** A painted alarm is a disabled alarm.

## 6. The furnace circuit and the disconnect (LICENSED)

A gas furnace on a 15-amp circuit, a heat pump on a 30- or 40-amp, a tankless water heater at 120–180 amps for whole-house. If the breaker is warm or the wire at the breaker is discoloured, that is a loose termination and it is a licensed repair before it becomes a fire.

**The thermostat wire is low voltage** and is a DIY swap if the wire colours match. A thermostat that needs a C wire for a smart unit may need a new run, which is licensed.

## 7. Christmas lights: the actual load rules (DIY, with care)

- **LED strings draw a fraction** of an incandescent set. Forty strands of LED can share an outdoor circuit; forty strands of incandescent will not.
- **Read the fuse in the plug.** A mini-string fuse is 3–5 amps and is there to fail before the wire does.
- **Outdoor connections:** no connection sits in a puddle, and every outdoor extension has a **GFCI** at the source.
- **Replace a string with a failed fuse once.** If the replacement blows, the string has a dead short and goes in the bin.

## 8. Surge protection at the panel, not just the strip (LICENSED for the panel)

A plug-in surge strip protects the things plugged into it, and mostly against small events. A **whole-home surge protective device** at the panel is a $300–$600 installed item and it protects the HVAC, the refrigerator, and the car charger, which is where the real claims are.

**In our climate,** the risk is not tropical storms, it is winter wind events and the utility switching. Both produce the same spike.

The **$30 strip still protects the electronics on it**, and both is the correct answer: a panel device for the house, strips for the TV and computer.

## 9. Test the exterior lights and doorway wiring (DIY for bulbs, LICENSED for runs)

- **Exterior receptacles** should be GFCI and in an in-use cover, which is the bubble that lets a plug stay in and still keep water out.
- **Doorbell transformers** buzz when they are failing, and the buzz is a licensed 30-minute fix.
- **A porch light that flickers in the wind** is a loose connection at the fixture, not a bad bulb.

## 10. The panel: a photograph and a map (DIY, no cover removal)

Photograph the open panel, write a new directory if the old one is illegible, and label every breaker by room and what it feeds. When a breaker trips at 9 p.m. in January, the directory is what gets the heat back on.

**Do not remove the panel deadfront** unless you are qualified. Standing in front of exposed bus bars in a panel is the highest-risk five minutes in residential electrical work.

**Signs the panel needs an electrician now:** a burning smell, discoloured breakers, a double-tapped breaker (two wires on one terminal on a non-tandem breaker), a federal Pacific or Zinsco panel, or a 60-amp service feeding a modern house.
