We assemble a lot of furniture. The part of the job people ask us to skip is the anchor strap, and it is the part that matters most.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission tracks furniture and television tip-over incidents at roughly **20,000 emergency department visits a year in the United States**, and the majority of the fatal cases are children under six. The pieces involved are not unusual: dressers, bookshelves, wardrobes, and TVs on stands.

## Why a dresser falls

A typical seven-drawer dresser holds 12 to 18 cubic feet of clothes and other weight, and the drawers slide on rails that shift that weight forward when opened. Open the top three drawers and most of the mass is now in front of the front feet. The tipping threshold for a full dresser is often a child's weight on an open drawer.

There is a second issue that surprises people: the anti-tip hardware included in the box is frequently a short strap with two small screws, sized for the cheapest possible assembly. It is not wrong, but it assumes the wall behind it is drywall over wood studs and that you can find a stud. Many anchors are installed into drywall only, which fails under a load that matters.

## Which pieces need anchoring

Anchor any of these if it is taller than it is wide, or if a drawer or door can be opened by a child:

- **All dressers and chests.** No exceptions. Every manufacturer now ships the strap for this reason.
- **Bookcases and shelving over 30 inches tall.**
- **Wardrobes and armoires**, especially with sliding doors that pull forward.
- **TVs on any stand**, and the stand itself if it has shelves.
- **Freestanding cabinet units** with a tip-over hazard label.

Chairs, sofas, and low coffee tables generally do not need it. The rule is leverage: if pulling the top toward you swings more of the object's weight forward of its front feet, it should be strapped.

## How to anchor it so it holds

1. **Find a stud or a top plate.** A stud finder plus a small nail through the drywall to confirm. A drywall toggle alone will hold a shelf but should not be the only thing restraining a full dresser.
2. **Replace the provided screws** with 2.5- to 3-inch structural screws that reach the framing, not the 3/4-inch screws in the bag.
3. **Anchor to the highest structural point on the back**, not the bottom. The higher the attachment, the shorter the lever arm when the piece does start to lean.
4. **Leave a small amount of slack**, roughly a quarter inch, so the strap does not tear the mount when the piece shifts slightly under load.
5. **Anchor both sides** on anything wider than about 30 inches, or use two straps.

If the wall is plaster, concrete, or the piece sits against a window, the good local solution is a strap into a stud on one side and a heavy toggle plus a rated anchor on the other. We carry both on the truck and will not install one into drywall alone.

## The assembly details that fail quietly

While the piece is apart, three things are worth checking, because they are the failure points we see on callbacks:

- **Cam locks not seated.** Most flat-pack assembly failures are a cam bolt that was tightened before the panel was fully seated, or a cam turned the wrong way. The lock should bottom out with a quarter turn, not spin.
- **Back panels.** The thin hardboard back is what keeps a bookcase square. If it is stapled only at the corners, add a screw every six inches along the perimeter. This is also what makes a shelf stable when it leans.
- **Weight rating.** Note the manufacturer's per-shelf rating, usually 25 to 50 pounds, and keep it. A shelf loaded past the rating bows the back panel and puts load into the cam joints.
