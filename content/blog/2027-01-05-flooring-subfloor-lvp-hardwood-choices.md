Two floors can cost the same per square foot and perform nothing alike, because the difference is not the plank. It is what is under it.

Luxury vinyl plank and hardwood both fail for the same three reasons: an out-of-flat subfloor, moisture, and movement that has nowhere to go. Get those three right and either product will last decades. Get them wrong and a $6 per square foot plank fails in two years.

## Flat is a number, not a feeling

**This is the spec that matters most.** Nearly every LVP manufacturer requires the subfloor flat within **3/16 inch over a 10-foot span**, and 1/8 inch over 6 feet; check the specific installation instructions, because they vary and the warranty is written against them.

A 1/4-inch dip over 10 feet is invisible to the eye and enough to make an LVP joint open, or to make a hardwood plank rock and squeak.

**Check it properly:** lay a 10-foot straight edge (a straight board or a level) across the room in at least four directions, and slide a feeler gauge or a set of coins under it. Note where it is high and where it is low, in feet.

- **Low spots up to 1/4 inch:** a patching compound, applied in lifts per the manufacturer's instruction.
- **High spots:** grind. Concrete can be ground with a rented grinder; a wood subfloor can be planed or sanded.
- **More than 1/4 inch of variation:** a self-leveling underlayment, priced per bag and worth the labour.

Skipping this step is the number one reason a floating floor sounds hollow and a click-lock joint opens at the seam six months in.

## Deflection: the floor under the floor

A hardwood floor needs a **joist deflection of L/360 or better**; stone tile needs L/720. That is not a material property, it is a structural one.

**Quick check:** bounce on the middle of the span while someone holds a glass of water on a shelf. If the water visibly ripples, the floor is too flexible for stone and possibly for hardwood. A crawl space with a long span and no midspan blocking is the usual culprit.

Fixing deflection means sistering joists or adding a beam. That is a structural conversation, and it is the one to have before the material is ordered, not after the first squeak.

## Moisture: the number that decides the product

Wood and vinyl both react to moisture, differently.

- **Concrete slab:** test for moisture emission. A calcium chloride test or a relative humidity test gives you a number; anything above the manufacturer's threshold means a vapor barrier and a specific adhesive, or no hardwood at all.
- **Wood subfloor over a crawl space:** the crawl space needs a vapor barrier on the ground and ventilation. A floor installed over a damp crawl space will cup.
- **Kitchen and bath:** a wet area needs a substrate that tolerates it. Cement backer board or a proper waterproofing membrane for tile; LVP is fine if the joints are sealed per the manufacturer.

**In our climate,** winter interior humidity can drop under 20%, and a wood floor installed at 30% will gap at the boards in January and close in July. That is normal seasonal movement, and it is why the material must **acclimate in the room** for the manufacturer's stated period (often 48–72 hours, sometimes a week) before installation.

## Expansion gaps: the 1/4 inch nobody budgets for

Every floating floor and every hardwood floor needs a gap at every **vertical obstruction**: the wall on all four sides, every cabinet leg, every door jamb, every pipe penetrating the floor.

A 1/4-inch gap covered by baseboard and shoe mold is invisible. A floor installed tight to the wall in a dry month will buckle in the summer when it expands. This is the most common cause of a "the floor is lifting in the middle of the room" call, and it is a $0 fix done correctly at install.

**Door jambs are undercut, not notched.** A jamb saw sets the cut to the thickness of the plank plus underlayment, and the plank slides under.

## LVP versus hardwood, the honest comparison

| | Luxury vinyl plank | Engineered hardwood | Solid hardwood |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wet areas | Excellent | Good with sealing | Poor in a bath |
| Refinishable | No | 1–3 times | 3–6 times |
| Subfloor tolerance | Strict flatness, tolerates minor moisture | Strict flatness | Needs a structural floor |
| Seasonal movement | Minimal | Moderate | High |
| Typical installed cost | $4–$8 / sq ft | $8–$14 / sq ft | $10–$20 / sq ft |
| Lifespan | 15–25 years | 25–40 years | 50+ years |
| Under a heavy island | Fine | Check deflection | Check deflection |

**The short answer:** LVP in a kitchen, a bath, a basement, or a rental. Engineered hardwood in living areas where you want to refinish once. Solid hardwood where the subfloor and the budget support it, and not in a bathroom.

## Transitions and level changes

- **Same height, two floors:** a T-mold.
- **Different heights:** a reducer or a threshold, matched to the material at each side.
- **Concrete to wood:** a reducer, and check the height before the wood is installed, because a 3/8-inch proud edge is a trip hazard.

Measure the height difference with a straight edge before buying a transition. The molding is the last thing you want to improvise.

## Squeaks: fix before the new floor, not after

A squeak is usually a subfloor panel moving against a joist, or two panels rubbing at a joint. Once the finished floor is down, a squeak is a drilling job from below or not fixable at all.

Before installation: screw every subfloor panel down on a 6-inch grid at the joists, with screws long enough to bite but not so long they puncture a pipe. That is the cheapest insurance in the whole project.

## The sequence that avoids rework

1. Test flatness and moisture. Write the numbers down.
2. Fix deflection if it is a problem. Now, not later.
3. Screw down and patch the subfloor.
4. Acclimate the material in the room.
5. Install with expansion gaps at every obstruction.
6. Undercut jambs with a jamb saw.
7. Transition moldings last, matched to measured heights.
