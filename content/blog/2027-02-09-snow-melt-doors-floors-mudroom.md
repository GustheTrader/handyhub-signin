The week after a storm is when the calls come in: the back door will not close, the floor by the door has cupped, and the baseboard is soft. All three are the same problem, water getting past the door, and the fixes are in a specific order.

## The exterior door is a weather system, not a slab

A door is a slab, a frame, a threshold, and a sweep. It keeps weather out only when all four work together.

**The three failures, in order of how often we see them:**

1. **The sweep is gone.** The rubber or brush bottom seal dries and compresses. It is a $20 part and a fifteen-minute swap on most doors.
2. **The threshold is not adjusted.** An adjustable threshold has screws from the bottom of the sill that raise a bar to meet the sweep. If the door drags on the threshold, lower it; if air and water pass, raise it.
3. **The frame has no seal at the sill corners.** Water comes in at the bottom corners where the jamb meets the sill, which is caulk, not weatherstripping.

**The door that swells shut in winter** is a wood door that has absorbed moisture at the bottom and has nowhere to go. Do not plane the door. Fix the water source and give it a week; the door opens again. Planing a swollen door turns into a gap in July.

## The one-way rule for snow melt

Snow melt goes **downhill and inward**. A door at a slab-on-grade entry with no step and no overhang gets water on the sill, and the sill is the path into the wall.

- **A 4-inch step or a threshold with a slope** keeps water off the door.
- **The overhang** is the cheapest protection. A small roof over an entry door keeps most of the snow off the door entirely.
- **The exterior siding should sit at least 6 inches above grade** and never touch the slab. A door installer who puts the threshold at the same height as the exterior soil guarantees a wet wall.

## The mudroom floor: what to put down and what to never put down

**Never:** laminate, engineered hardwood, and solid hardwood in a mudroom with a door to snow. These floors cup and lift at the seams the first winter, and no sealant fixes a wet substrate under the board.

**Do:** porcelain tile, luxury vinyl plank rated for wet areas, or sealed concrete. Porcelain is the most forgiving and the most expensive to install. A wet-rated LVP over a sealed substrate is the practical middle.

- **A floor drain** in a mudroom is worth it if the floor can slope to it. It is the only fix that handles a boot full of snow melting in place.
- **A boot tray** with a lip, plus a rubber mat that does not trap water underneath. A mat that sits flat in a puddle is worse than no mat, because the water cannot dry.
- **The baseboard is the canary.** A soft baseboard or a swollen quarter round at the door means water has been reaching the wall for at least a season.

## Interior doors in winter: the 1/8-inch gaps

Dry winter air shrinks wood, and doors that fit in October show a gap in January.

- **A gap at the hinge side** is seasonal, and it closes in spring. Do not shim it.
- **A door that rattles** needs a strike plate adjustment, not a new door.
- **A door that sticks at the top corner** in winter is usually a hinge screw that has stripped. A longer screw into the stud, or a dowel and glue, fixes it.
- **The 1/8-inch reveal** around a door should be even. If it is uneven top to bottom, the frame is out of square and the hinges need to be reset.

**The latch that will not catch** is almost always a strike plate that sits a millimetre too far in or out. A file on the plate, or a plate adjustment, is a five-minute fix.

## Sliding doors and the snow track

- **Clean the track** with a vacuum and a brush, then wipe the rollers. Grit in the track is what makes a slider hard to open, not the rollers.
- **The weep holes** in the bottom of the slider frame must be open. A plugged weep hole means water stands in the track and comes inside over the threshold.
- **Adjust the rollers:** a slider has an adjustment screw at the bottom of each roller. Raise or lower it to make the door close evenly.
- **The screen door** should be adjusted separately. A slider that will not latch is usually a screen that has dropped.

## The order to fix it

1. **Stop the water first.** Sweep, threshold, and caulk at the sill corners.
2. **Check the grade and the overhang.** Water that reaches the door beats any seal.
3. **Dry the floor and the wall** before any flooring repair. A floor over a wet substrate fails again.
4. **Replace the flooring** only after the water path is closed.
5. **Adjust the interior doors** last, and note the gaps so you know next January what is seasonal.
