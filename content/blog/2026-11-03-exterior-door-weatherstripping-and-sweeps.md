Reno/Sparks sits in one of the windiest corridors in the West. When the gusts come through in the fall, an exterior door that leaks air is not just uncomfortable. It is the largest uncontrolled hole in your heating bill.

The good news: it is one of the few repairs where the cost of doing it properly is genuinely small, and the difference is visible in the first week.

## The three seals, not one

People weatherstrip the jamb and stop, then wonder why the door still whistles. An exterior door is a three-part seal:

1. **The jamb seal** (the sides and top). This is what most kits replace.
2. **The door sweep or shoe** at the bottom. This is what fails first and gets replaced least often, because you never see it.
3. **The threshold and its seal.** Old sills with a worn vinyl insert, or a gap between the sill and the slab, leak directly into the house.

A door with a good sweep and a failed jamb seal leaks about as much as the reverse. Both have to work.

## Find the leak without guessing

With the door closed and the latch catching, on a windy day:

- **Smoke pen or incense** run slowly around the perimeter, at the hinges, across the bottom, and at the threshold ends. Follow the smoke; it will bend at the leak.
- **A dollar bill test** at three points along the latch side: top, middle, bottom. If the bill slides out with no resistance at any point, the door is not meeting the stop there.
- **A light test at night.** Put someone outside with a flashlight and look for a line of light. This is the most reliable test and it costs nothing.

Where you find the leak determines the repair. Air at the bottom is a sweep or threshold. Air along the middle of the latch side means the door is warped or the hinge is not aligned. Air at the top corner means the stop is loose or the header is not true.

Doing all three seals at once is cheaper than doing them one at a time, because the truck is already there. If your door needs two of the three, do the third while the door is off the hinges.

## The reverse: doors that stick

The same door that leaks air in January can drag on the carpet in July. Warm weather expands the door slab and the frame together, and a door that was planed flush in winter binds in summer.

The fix is to remove material from the **top or latch edge**, not the bottom, unless the bottom is what is dragging. Planing the bottom is the common DIY move and the wrong one when the drag is along the latch side, because it opens a gap that leaks for the other nine months.

Also check for a sagging hinge. A single hinge screw that has pulled out of the jamb lets the door drop and drag at the strike plate. A longer screw into the framing behind the jamb, not a toothpick and glue, is the permanent repair.

## One more thing to inspect while you are there

Look at the threshold on the inside. If you can see daylight at either end, water will come in sideways and rot the subfloor. In this climate, the damage from a leaking threshold shows up as a soft spot just inside the door and a dark stain on the sill plate below. That is a real repair, not a weatherstripping job, and it is worth catching before the first snow.
