People choose flooring on the finish and the price. The floor that fails in this climate fails for a different reason: what is underneath it, and what the air is doing to it.

## The one number that decides everything: flatness

Every floating floor has a flatness tolerance, and it is measured over 10 feet.

- **Laminate:** 3/16 inch over 10 feet.
- **LVP (luxury vinyl plank):** 1/8 inch over 10 feet on most products, sometimes 3/16.
- **Glue-down or nailed hardwood:** 3/16 inch over 10 feet, and stricter at the seams.

The trap is that a subfloor can be flat enough for carpet and not close for laminate. Carpet absorbs a 1/4-inch dip. A rigid plank layer over it acts as a bridge, and the bridge is what clicks, cups, and fails at the joints within two winters.

**Measuring it is a ten-minute job.** A 10-foot straightedge and a flashlight. Set the straightedge on the floor and look for light under the middle. More than 3/16 inch and the floor needs leveling compound or a new underlayment, and that is a line item worth seeing in a quote before the material is ordered, not after.

## Why laminate fails where LVP survives

Laminate is a fiberboard core with a printed film and a wear layer. The core is the problem. A spill that sits for an hour swells the edge, and a swollen edge does not recover. This is why laminate is fine in a living room and wrong in a laundry room, under a dishwasher, or anywhere a pet drinks.

LVP has a waterproof core, either SPC (stone plastic composite) or WPC (wood plastic composite). A spill on the surface is not an event. That does not make it waterproof at the perimeter: water that runs under the planks and sits against drywall still causes problems, and a flooded dishwasher leak still needs the floor pulled.

## The Reno moisture problem

The dominant failure mode here is not water, it is dry air. Indoor relative humidity falls into the teens in January, and wood gives up moisture and shrinks. A solid hardwood floor that had no movement gap at the wall will open gaps at the seams, and those gaps do not close all the way back up in July.

The fixes are unglamorous and they work:

- **Leave the expansion gap.** 1/4 to 3/8 inch at every wall, hidden under baseboard. A floor installed tight to a wall will buckle in summer. This is the single most common installation mistake we are called to fix.
- **Hold 35–55% indoor humidity.** A $60 hygrometer tells you where you are. A whole-house humidifier is the real answer, and it does more for wood floors, wood cabinets, and drywall seams than almost any other home upgrade.
- **Let material acclimate.** A plank floor should sit in the room it goes in for 48–72 hours before installation. Cold-delivered material from a garage goes down at the wrong dimension.
- **Engineered over solid, above grade.** Engineered hardwood tolerates humidity swings better because the cross-ply core resists dimensional movement.

## Transition strips and door undercuts

A flooring change at a doorway needs either a **T-molding** over a level transition or a **reducer** where heights differ. Both need the door undercut to clear. A door that drags after a floor job is a 20-minute fix with a door saw, and it should be in the scope, not a second visit.

Check under the door before material is ordered. A 1-3/4 inch door with 5/8 inch clearance fits an 8mm plank with underlayment. A thicker SPC plank with a 3mm pad may not, and that means either undercutting every door or choosing a thinner product.

## What to ask before you buy

1. **What is the flatness spec on this product,** and has anyone measured my floor?
2. **What is the underlayment,** and is it included in the pad or a separate vapor barrier?
3. **Where is the expansion gap** and will it be covered by the existing baseboard?
4. **Does the door clear** the finished height at every threshold?
5. **Who warranties it,** the manufacturer or the installer, and does the warranty survive DIY installation? Most do not.
