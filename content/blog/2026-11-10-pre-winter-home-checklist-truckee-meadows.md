Reno and Sparks get their first hard freeze most years in the last week of November or the first week of December. Nights drop into the teens and the daytime high does not always get back above freezing.

The damage from a freeze almost never happens to the things people worry about. It happens in a fixed order, and every item on the list below is cheap.

## The order things break

**1. An exterior hose bib with a hose still attached.** A hose left connected holds water in the bib's body, and the supply line behind it splits. This is the single most common freeze damage we repair, and the resulting interior leak can run down inside a wall for hours before anyone notices.

**2. Drip irrigation and sprinkler lines.** A backflow preventer or zone valve left under pressure cracks its housing. The break shows up in April, when you turn the system on and the water comes out of the side of the valve instead of the heads.

**3. A slab-on-grade plumbing run.** Homes with a supply line running through the attic, common in some builds, are worse off than a hose bib. An attic line that freezes bursts into the ceiling.

**4. The door thresholds.** This is not a water problem, it is a heat loss and a damage problem. A door with a worn threshold seal lets snowmelt in, and the water sits on the flooring. That is how a three-year-old floor gets a delaminated edge in one winter.

**5. Gutters and downspouts.** A downspout that discharges at the foundation sends freeze-thaw water against the slab, and water standing against a foundation wall is what heaves a driveway and wicks into a crawl space.

## The checklist, in the order to do it

**Before the first freeze night:**

- **Disconnect every hose.** Empty it and store it. If a hose is frozen to the bib, do not force it.
- **Shut off and drain the hose bibs.** Find the interior shutoff, usually a valve a foot or two inside the wall, close it, then open the exterior faucet to drain the stub. If you cannot find a shutoff, a foam bib cover is the fallback at $6, and it is not as good.
- **Blow out the irrigation system.** Compressed air through the zone valves, one zone at a time, until only mist comes out. This is the one item worth paying for: $85–$150 for a standard system, and it prevents a $400 valve replacement plus the repair to the line.
- **Close the crawl space vents.** Winter-only. Note it so you can reopen them in spring, because a closed crawl space in July is a mold problem.
- **Replace the furnace filter** and note the size. A dirty filter in the first cold week is the usual cause of a high-limit trip.

**Before the second week of cold:**

- **Walk every exterior door** with the lights on inside and the door closed. Look for light along the threshold, the jambs, and the head. A dollar bill should drag when you close the door on it. If it slides through freely, the weatherstripping is gone.
- **Check the threshold sweep** at the bottom. If it is a metal sweep with a rubber insert, the insert is replaceable on many models without replacing the door, for $15–$40.
- **Test every exterior light and the doorbell.** Ice on a fixture housing is a cracked housing by spring.
- **Look at the gutters from the ground.** A gutter that has pulled away from the fascia holds water and ice and pulls further.
- **Test the smoke and CO detectors** and check the expiration date on the CO units, which have a 5–7 year sensor life.
- **Test the GFCI protecting the exterior and garage outlets**, from the farthest outlet, not just the button. Pump pads and exterior outlets trip in wet weather and, if the device has failed, nothing downstream is protected.
