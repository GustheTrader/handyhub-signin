Most electrical fires start at a connection, not in a wire, and the connection usually gives warning long before it burns. This is the walkthrough we do on arrival, and it is worth doing on your own house once a year. It takes about five minutes per room and it needs no tools beyond a pen and a nightlight.

**Non-negotiable:** if you see scorching, smell burning plastic, feel heat at a cover plate, or hear buzzing, stop. Turn that circuit off and call. Nothing below requires opening a panel or removing a cover.

## 1. Test every GFCI and reset it

A GFCI has two buttons: **TEST** and **RESET**. Press test. It should click, and the outlet should dead. Press reset and it should return.

- **Location:** kitchen counters, bathrooms, garage, exterior, laundry, unfinished basement, and any outlet within 6 feet of a sink.
- **The test is the whole point.** A GFCI that will not reset is tripped or failed; a GFCI that will not test has a failed internal mechanism and is not protecting anything.
- **A GFCI protects everything downstream on the same circuit.** That is why one dead bathroom outlet can mean the reset is in another bathroom, or in the garage.
- **The one in the garage** is the most commonly overlooked reset on the whole house. If outdoor outlets are dead, check the garage first.

## 2. Test the AFCI breakers

Arc-fault breakers, usually on bedrooms and living areas, have a test button on the breaker itself. Pressing it should trip the breaker. This is the one breaker test you do at the panel, and it does not require removing the cover.

An AFCI is designed for the failure mode you cannot see: a loose connection arcing inside a wall.

## 3. Read the panel label out loud

Find the panel and read the directory aloud. If you cannot say what a breaker controls, that label is wrong, and the next person who needs to kill a circuit in an emergency pays for it.

- **Label in plain language:** "Kitchen counter outlets, east wall" beats "K-2".
- **Walk the house with a nightlight** and a helper on a phone call. Plug the light in, flip one breaker, note which rooms went dark.
- **Photograph the finished panel** and keep it on your phone. It is faster than reading a label in the dark.
- **Note the main breaker and the meter location.** Everyone in the house should know where the main is.

## 4. Count the load on the breakers

The 80% rule: a **15-amp circuit is 1,800 watts** and a **20-amp is 2,400 watts**, but a continuous load should stay at **80% of that: 1,440 and 1,920 watts**.

A space heater is the number to know:

- **1,500-watt space heater = 12.5 amps** on 120 volts. That is 83% of a 15-amp circuit by itself.
- **Two space heaters on one circuit** is a guaranteed trip at best and a warm cord at worst.
- **A bathroom heater, a hair dryer, and a light** on one 15-amp circuit is the same problem.

**The practical rule:** one heat-producing appliance per circuit, and space heaters on their own circuit if the house allows it.

## 5. Look at the outlets you already have

You do not need to open anything. You need to look and touch.

- **Warm cover plate** after an appliance has run: a loose connection behind it. This is the one you should not ignore.
- **Discoloured or brown outlet face:** heat. Replace the outlet.
- **A plug that falls out** or a plug that is hard to insert: worn contacts, which is a fire path. Replace the receptacle, not the appliance cord.
- **Cracked faceplates** in a bathroom or kitchen are a water path. Replace them.
- **Ungrounded three-prong outlets** in an older house: check with a $10 outlet tester. An ungrounded outlet labelled "grounded" by a previous owner is a hazard.
- **Outlets in a wet location with no cover** need a bubble cover, not a flat plate.

## 6. Extension cords, power strips, and the daisy chain

**The rule:** an extension cord is temporary. It is not a substitute for a circuit.

- **Never** a power strip plugged into an extension cord plugged into a power strip. This is the single most common overload path.
- **Never** a space heater on an extension cord. The cord is rated for the heater or it is not, and most are not.
- **Gauge:** a 16-gauge cord will not carry a 15-amp load over 25 feet without getting warm. Use 12-gauge for anything that heats.
- **Cords under carpets and rugs** are crushed, and a crushed cord is an arc.
- **Frayed or taped cords** get replaced. A taped cord is a cord you have already decided is broken.

## 7. Smoke and CO alarms

- **Smoke alarm on every level, in every bedroom, and outside each sleeping area.**
- **CO alarm** near the furnace and outside the sleeping area.
- **Test monthly, replace the unit every 10 years.** The date is printed on the back. A 2012 unit is done.
- **Battery:** a chirping alarm is a low battery, not a nuisance. Replace it the same day.

## 8. The panel is warm, the breakers are old

- **A warm panel cover** is normal in a load centre. A **hot spot** on a cover plate is not.
- **A breaker that trips repeatedly** is a fault, not a nuisance trip. Do not upsize the breaker to stop it.
- **Zinsco and Federal Pacific Stab-Lok panels** have a known failure history. If the house has one, an electrician's inspection is the right call, and we will say so plainly.
- **A double-tapped breaker** (two wires on one screw) is common in older houses and is allowed only for certain breakers. It is not a DIY fix.

## The five-minute version

**Test one GFCI in each bathroom, test the garage GFCI, press each AFCI test button, read three panel labels aloud, and touch the cover plate on the outlet behind the space heater.** That is the walkthrough. It finds most of what matters.

**This week's recommendation:** go press TEST on the GFCI in the bathroom you use most. If it does not click and go dead, it has not been protecting that outlet, and that is a today problem, not a spring problem.
