A kitchen outlet that trips when the coffee maker and the griddle are on together is not failing. It is working exactly as designed, and the design assumed you would not do that.

What is failing is the assumption that one 20-amp circuit is enough for a modern counter.

## What the code actually requires

The National Electrical Code, adopted statewide in Nevada, sets three requirements for kitchens that homeowners ask about constantly:

- **NEC 210.8(A)(6)**: all 125-volt, 15- and 20-amp receptacles serving **countertop surfaces** need GFCI protection. That includes the one behind the fridge if it is a counter-height outlet, and it includes older homes where the outlets were done before this was standard.
- **NEC 210.52(C)(1)**: a **small appliance branch circuit** serving countertop receptacles must be 20 amp, and a minimum of **two** such circuits are required for a kitchen.
- **NEC 210.52(B)(2)**: those small appliance circuits may not serve anything else. Not the dishwasher, not the disposal, not the range hood, not the dining room outlet.

That second clause is where most Truckee Meadows homes built before the mid-1990s fall short. A two-circuit kitchen was common, but so was tapping the dining room or the fridge onto one of them. Every appliance added since then competes with the same 20 amps.

## Why "just reset it" is not the fix

A GFCI trips on a 5-milliamp imbalance, a 20-amp breaker trips on current. They are different failures and they point at different things.

- **GFCI button pops, nothing else in the room went dark**: usually a wet connection, a failed outlet, or a bad GFCI in the middle of the string. Reset it; if it pops again with nothing plugged in, the outlet or the downstream wiring is the problem.
- **Breaker trips (the switch in the panel, not the button)**: an overload. Two heating appliances on one circuit do this every time. Count it: a coffee maker is 1,500 watts, a toaster 1,200, a griddle 1,500, an air fryer 1,700. Two of anything crosses 20 amps at 120 volts. There is no reset that fixes arithmetic.
- **Trips only when the dishwasher runs**: a shared circuit, which is the code violation above.

Fishing a circuit through a finished kitchen is the cost driver, not the wire. If you are already opening a wall for a backsplash or a cabinet change, that is the cheapest moment you will ever have to add a circuit, and it is worth asking for it in the same visit.

## The appliance-vs-circuit test

Before calling anyone, unplug everything on the counter and plug the appliances in one at a time, then in pairs. If it trips on a specific pair, you have your answer: overload. If it trips alone on one appliance, the appliance is the suspect. If it trips with nothing plugged in, the wiring or the outlet is the suspect.

Ten minutes of that saves you a diagnostic fee.

## Older wiring worth a second look

If your home predates the early 1980s and you have never seen a GFCI in the kitchen, a device called an **AFCI/GFCI combination breaker** is likely a worthwhile upgrade in the panel. It catches the arc faults that come from aging aluminum-branch connections and the ground faults from a wet countertop, in one thirty-dollar part. This requires being inside the panel, which is not a homeowner job, so plan it as a scheduled visit rather than a weekend project.
