A breaker that keeps tripping is doing its job. The question is whether it is protecting you from a fault you cannot see, or whether it is a tired device protecting a wiring run that no longer needs it.

The distinction matters because resetting a breaker that keeps tripping without finding the cause is how a house gets a fire.

## What the two devices actually do

**A GFCI** protects people. It trips when current leaves the circuit through something other than the hot and neutral: a person, or water, or a degraded cord. It trips at 5 milliamps, which is well below the level that stops a heart. The test button on the outlet tests the device itself, not the wiring downstream.

**An AFCI** protects the structure. It detects the electrical signature of an arc, a loose connection or a damaged cord that sparks. Code has required AFCI protection in bedrooms since 1999 and in most of the house since 2014, and Reno/Sparks homes built after that are covered.

Neither one trips because the outlet is worn. If it trips, something downstream is the reason.

## Isolating a tripping circuit in ten minutes

1. **Unplug everything** on the circuit. Not switched off, unplugged.
2. **Reset the breaker or the GFCI.** Note whether it trips immediately with nothing plugged in.
3. **If it trips with nothing plugged in**, the fault is in the wiring or a device on the run. Stop here and call an electrician. This is the one case that is never a user problem.
4. **If it holds**, plug things back in one at a time, testing the reset each time. The item that trips it is the fault, whether a hair dryer, a space heater, a fridge, or a lamp with a nicked cord.
5. **If two things trip it together but neither does alone**, you are near the circuit's capacity. That is an overload, not a fault, and it needs a dedicated circuit.

An outlet that is **downstream of a GFCI** and has no power is often not broken. One GFCI can protect up to six outlets after it. Check every GFCI in the house, including the one in the garage and the one behind a shelf, before booking a repair. A dead bathroom outlet is a tripped garage GFCI more often than it is a failed device.

## The GFCI that will not reset

**A GFCI that clicks but does not hold has a real fault downstream.** It is not a failed device. Something on the protected run is leaking current to ground, typically a cheap power strip, a cord, or an appliance with moisture in it.

**A GFCI that has no power at all** may be a dead device. These have a useful life of about 10–15 years, and they fail closed on older ones. If yours is from the 1990s and the trip button feels soft or the outlet does not reset even with everything unplugged, replace it, and note that the replacement is $25–$35 in parts.

**Weather-resistant GFCIs in an exterior box** are the exception where an old device is the likely cause. Exterior units are exposed to temperature swings and their internal components fail years before an indoor unit.

## Signs that mean stop and call, today

- **Burning smell** anywhere near an outlet or panel, or a scorched faceplate.
- **A warm outlet faceplate.** Warm is a problem. Devices should be room temperature.
- **Buzz or flicker** on a circuit, especially when a large load starts.
- **A breaker that trips when the circuit is completely empty.**
- **Aluminum branch wiring** in a 1960s–70s home, when adding a device.
- **A panel labeled with handwriting and questions.** Federal Pacific Stab-Lok panels in particular get an electrician's attention, and a replacement is $1,800–$3,500.

None of the above is something to reset and watch.

## The habit worth building

Label the panel. Every breaker gets an index card or a printed label that says what it controls, not "lights." When a circuit trips at 9pm and the panel is anonymous, you reset things until something works and you never find the cause. With labels, isolation takes one minute.

And test the GFCIs. Push the test button once a month, because a GFCI that trips on its own test is one you can trust later. The button that has never been pressed is the one that will not trip when it matters.
