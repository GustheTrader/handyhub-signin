Every item below is a thing we get called about in January that could have been a twenty-minute job in November. Work down the list in order; the first five are the ones that stop a five-figure repair.

## 1. The furnace filter, the thermostat, and the CO alarm

**Filter:** replace a 1-inch filter every 1–3 months in heating season, more often with pets. A clogged filter is the single most common cause of a furnace short-cycling.

**Thermostat:** set it to **68 °F** while occupied and **55 °F** when away. Below 55 °F, pipes in an exterior wall become a risk.

**CO alarm:** a combination smoke/CO alarm near the furnace and one outside the sleeping area. A cracked heat exchanger produces CO with no smoke and no smell. The alarm is the only warning you get.

**Furnace service:** one annual clean and inspect runs $100–$200, and it is the difference between a service call in November and a no-heat call on the coldest night of the year.

## 2. Weatherstripping, door thresholds, and the dollar test

Close a door on a dollar bill and pull. If the bill slides out freely, the seal is gone.

- **Door sweeps** for the gap under the door, with a new threshold if the sweep does not reach.
- **Foam or V-strip weatherstripping** in the jamb, replaced when it is compressed flat.
- **A door that has shifted** so the gap changes along the opening needs a hinge adjustment, not more weatherstripping.
- **Windows:** check the sash lock. A locked window is pulled tighter at the meeting rail and seals better; a lot of winter window drafts are an unlocked window.

**A $2 tube of exterior caulk** on the gaps at the window and door trim closes the path that weatherstripping cannot reach.

## 3. Pipes, hose bibs, and the shut-off the builder forgot

- **Disconnect every garden hose.** A hose left on a hose bib traps water in the pipe, freezes, splits the bib, and floods the basement on the first thaw.
- **Shut off and drain hose bibs** if they have an interior valve.
- **Find the main shut-off** and tag it. Everyone should be able to close it in ten seconds in an emergency.
- **Insulate pipes in the garage, the crawl space, and any exterior wall.** A foam pipe sleeve is $2 per six feet.
- **Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls** during a hard freeze, so warm room air reaches the pipes.
- **The drip rule:** during a hard freeze, a pencil-lead-diameter trickle on a vulnerable faucet keeps the line moving. Moving water does not freeze as readily.

**Signs a pipe has frozen:** a faucet with no flow, a frost line on the pipe, a slight bulge. **Shut off the main and call before you thaw with a torch.** A torch on a frozen pipe is the second emergency.

## 4. Attic insulation, the bypass, and the hatch

- **The target** in our climate is roughly **R-38 to R-49** in the attic, which is 12–16 inches of fibreglass.
- **The attic hatch or door** is usually the weakest point. An insulated hatch cover with a foam gasket fixes it in an afternoon.
- **Air sealing beats adding insulation.** A gap at a top plate, a recessed light, or a chimney chase moves more heat than an inch of missing insulation.
- **Do not cover the soffit vents** with insulation; a vent chute keeps them open.
- **Check for a soffit vent that is blocked** by a bath fan or a dryer duct, which is the most common attic bypass we find.

## 5. Roof, gutters, and the ice dam

- **Clear the gutters before the snow.** Water in a full gutter freezes and the weight pulls the gutter off the fascia.
- **Downspouts** should discharge 4–6 feet from the foundation, not onto it.
- **Look at the roof from the ground,** not from the roof. Granule loss, lifted shingles, and cracked flashing at the chimney.
- **Ice dams** form when warm attic air melts snow and it refreezes at the eave. The cure is attic air sealing and insulation, not a roof rake. A rake is a temporary fix.
- **Never put a ladder on ice.** Roof work in winter is a licensed trade and a warm day job.

## 6. Exterior: caulk, paint, and the small openings

- **Caulk every penetration:** hose bibs, electrical boxes, dryer vents, and pipes through siding.
- **Dryer vent** and any vent flap: check that it closes and is not torn.
- **Cracked exterior paint** lets water into the substrate. Paint is cheap, siding is not.
- **Deck:** a deck that has pulled away from the ledger or has loose fasteners should be looked at before it holds snow.

## 7. Snow tools, ice, and the roof-load number

- **Snow shovel** for walkways and a **roof rake** only for a low-slope section you can reach from the ground.
- **Ice melt:** calcium chloride works at lower temperatures than rock salt and is gentler on concrete. Sodium chloride damages walks and kills plantings.
- **Roof load:** wet snow is roughly 15–20 lb per cubic foot, and ice about 57 lb per cubic foot. A foot of wet snow on a roof is a real load. Clear it if the forecast adds more.

## 8. Generator, cord, and carbon monoxide

- **Portable generator outside, at least 20 feet from any door, window, or vent.** Never in the garage, even with the door open.
- **CO alarm** in the room next to the generator's exhaust path.
- **Cord gauge** matters: a 12-gauge cord for a 15-amp heater. A 16-gauge extension on a space heater is a fire.
- **Test the generator** before the storm, not during it.

## 9. Emergency kit the region actually needs

Flashlights and batteries, a battery radio, water for three days (one gallon per person per day), a full tank of fuel, phone chargers and a power bank, a first aid kit, blankets, a manual can opener, and copies of important documents.

**Add snow-specific:** ice scraper, a shovel in each car, a blanket and boots in each car, and a charged phone. Our storm risk is snow and wind, not hurricanes, and the stores empty of the same four things every year.
