The repairs that cost the most are almost never the ones that look urgent. They are the $60 part that fails quietly for two years. Here are nine jobs under $500, ordered by how much water damage they prevent.

## 1. Get the ground sloping away, and the downspouts to discharge

**One inch of fall per foot of run** for the first six feet away from the foundation. That is the whole rule.

- **Downspouts discharge 4–6 feet from the foundation**, not onto it and not into a splash block sitting in a low spot.
- **A buried downspout extension** to a pop-up emitter costs $40–$80 and is the single best water-management dollar you will spend.
- **A window well** without a drain or cover fills and pushes water into the basement. A clear polycarbonate cover is $60–$120.
- **The soil under a deck** should slope away too. Standing water under a deck rots the ledger.

**Why this beats a sump pump:** a sump pump is a response to water arriving. Gradient stops it arriving.

## 2. The toilet: wax ring, supply line, and flange

A toilet leaks about 200 gallons a year silently around a failed wax ring. The evidence is a rusted flange bolt, a rocking bowl, or a soft subfloor at the base.

- **A toilet that rocks** needs a new wax ring and shims. A rocking toilet stresses the wax ring until it fails.
- **A supply line older than 10 years** is $10–$20 for braided stainless and a five-minute swap. A burst toilet supply is a whole-floor flood.
- **The flange** should sit flush with the finished floor. A flange below the floor needs an extension kit.
- **A shut-off valve that does not close** behind the toilet or under the sink means a small leak becomes a flood because you cannot stop the water.

Replace every under-sink and toilet supply shut-off you cannot turn by hand. That is a $15 part and the most valuable ten minutes in this post.

## 3. Bath fan: airflow, duct, and humidity

A bath fan that runs but does not clear the mirror is moving no air.

- **The target is 1 CFM per square foot of bathroom**, minimum 50 CFM. A 100-square-foot bathroom needs a 100 CFM fan.
- **The test:** hold a piece of toilet paper against the grille. It should hold. If it falls, the fan or the duct is blocked.
- **Duct must be insulated** in the attic, or it condenses in winter and drips.
- **Duct must terminate outside**, not into the attic. A fan ducted into the attic puts the moisture in the coldest space in the house.
- **Flexible duct** should be as short and straight as possible. Every bend is airflow lost.
- **Timer switch** ($25–$45) so the fan runs 20 minutes after the shower. A fan switched off with the light does nothing.

**The number that matters:** a relative humidity above 60% in a bathroom grows mould. A $20 humidity gauge in the bathroom tells you whether the fan is doing its job.

## 4. Caulk and grout, done right

- **Caulk in the corners, grout on the field.** Caulk at a change of plane (where wall meets tub, where wall meets floor), because those joints move. Grout there cracks.
- **Remove the old caulk entirely** before new. Caulk over caulk fails in a season.
- **Silicone in wet areas**, paintable latex where paint meets trim. Silicone does not take paint.
- **Grout sealer** on tile floors and in showers, reapplied yearly in a shower. A shower that will not dry between uses needs a squeegee, not more sealer.
- **The test for a wet shower wall:** press a thumb into the grout line. If it crumbles, the substrate has been wet and the repair is bigger than caulk.

## 5. Deck hardware and the ledger

- **Check every fastener** with a screwdriver. A loose deck board screw gets replaced with a coated structural screw, not a drywall screw.
- **The ledger board** where the deck attaches to the house is the failure point. Look for rust streaks and soft wood. Flashing above the ledger is required, and missing flashing is common.
- **Railings:** a railing should resist a 200-pound push in any direction. A railing that moves is a fall risk, and that is a today job.
- **Staining:** clean, dry, then stain. Staining wet wood traps moisture and the finish peels in a year.

## 6. Gutters, and when to stop cleaning them

- **Cleaning is $150–$350** a year, twice a year if there are trees.
- **Gutter guards** at $200–$600 for a typical run stop the leaves but not the small debris. They reduce cleaning to an inspection.
- **The seams** leak before the gutters overflow. A gutter that drips at a seam is a corner of the foundation getting water.
- **A gutter that has pulled away from the fascia** means the spike and ferrule has failed; a hidden hanger is the fix.

## 7. Doors, windows, and the smoke alarm battery

- **Weatherstripping on every exterior door**, checked with the dollar-bill test.
- **The window that will not stay open** needs a sash balance or a channel repair, not a prop.
- **A sliding window that will not lock** is a security issue with a cheap fix: the lock and the keeper are adjustable.
- **Smoke and CO alarm batteries**, and the 10-year replacement date on the back of each unit.

## 8. The under-$100 electrical items

- **GFCI test on every protected outlet** (see our annual electrical walkthrough).
- **A whole-home surge protector** is a $300–$600 job, but a quality plug-in surge strip for the TV and network gear is $40–$80 and worth it.
- **Dimmer and switch plates** in a bathroom or kitchen that are cracked or discoloured: replace. $5 each.
- **LED bulbs in every fixture** so the fixture's rated wattage is never the limit.

## 9. The kitchen and bath items that hide a leak

- **Under-sink cabinet floor:** look for water staining and a swollen cabinet bottom. That is a leak, and a swollen MDF cabinet bottom is a repair, not a cleanup.
- **The refrigerator water line** and the dishwasher supply are the two most common slow leaks behind a cabinet.
- **A bathroom exhaust fan ducted to the wrong place** dumps humidity into the attic. That is the number one cause of attic mould we see.
- **A toilet that runs** between flushes wastes up to 200 gallons a day. A flapper is $8.

## The order that matters

1. **Water away from the house** (ground, downspouts, window wells).
2. **Water inside the house** (supply lines, shut-offs, wax ring, under-sink).
3. **Moisture in the air** (bath fan, dryer vent).
4. **Safety** (smoke and CO alarms, GFCI, railings).
5. **Cosmetics** (caulk, paint, grout) — last, because the substrate has to be dry before it gets sealed.
