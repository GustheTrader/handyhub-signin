A bathroom fan is usually undersized, vented to the wrong place, or both. The fix is inexpensive, and the damage it prevents is the kind that shows up in the ceiling below, or in a cabinet that swells and never closes right again.

## The CFM number your fan should hit

The rule of thumb is **1 CFM per square foot of bathroom floor**, with a minimum of 50 CFM for any bathroom.

A 5x8 bath is 40 sq ft, so it needs **50 CFM minimum**. A 10x10 master bath is 100 sq ft and needs **100 CFM**. Most builder fans are 50–80 CFM and most larger master baths are under-vented by half.

**A quick test:** hold a piece of tissue to the grille. If it does not stay up, the fan or the duct has a problem. This is not a precision test, but it separates a working fan from a decorative one.

**Long baths and long runs:** a fan on a 25-foot duct with four elbows is moving far less than its rating. A fan rated 110 CFM on a long run can deliver 70. The duct run matters as much as the fan.

## Where the duct goes, and where it usually goes wrong

The duct must terminate **outside**, through a wall or the roof, with a backdraft damper and a hood.

The three common failures:

1. **Venting into the attic.** This is the worst case. A bath pulls a pint to a quart of water per shower, and dumping it into an attic condenses on the roof deck and the insulation. The result is mould on the sheathing and, eventually, a rotted roof. This is a same-week fix.
2. **Venting into a soffit.** A soffit vent with a plastic flapper is often blocked by paint or stuck open. A soffit is an intake on a vented roof, so warm moist air pulled into the soffit goes straight back into the attic.
3. **A flexible duct crushed behind the drywall.** If the fan is loud and weak, the duct is pinched. A 4-inch flex run should be taut and short, and a long run should be rigid duct, insulated.

**Insulate the duct** in an unconditioned space, or it will sweat and drip.

## Why the fan should run past the shower

**The moisture is in the air after the shower, not during it.** A fan switched off with the light stops removing water exactly when the shower is dumping the most.

- **Run the fan 20 minutes after the shower,** ideally on a timer or a humidity sensor.
- **A humidity-sensing fan** is the correct upgrade. It starts at 60% RH and runs until the bath is back to a dry level.
- **A motion-sensing timer** covers the light and the fan with one switch.

This one change is the difference between a bath that smells faintly of damp after every shower and one that does not.

## The humidity target in winter

In our climate, outdoor air is dry and the bath is the wettest room in the house. The target:

- **Bathroom RH under 60%** after a shower. Above that, mould on the ceiling corner and around the window.
- **Whole-house winter RH of 30–40%.** Below 20% and the wood dries and the doors stick or gap; above 50% in cold weather and you get condensation on the glass, which is the version that rots a window sill.

**A $15 hygrometer** in the bath is the only measurement that tells you whether the fan is doing its job.

## Cabinets: why the boxes swell and the doors drag

Bath and kitchen cabinets fail at the **water line** and at the **floor line**.

- **Under-sink leaks:** any leak, even a slow weeper, wets the particleboard box. Particleboard swells about 10–15% and never returns to its original thickness, which is why a swollen cabinet floor stays lumpy.
- **The fix that lasts:** a plastic leak pan or a waterproof mat under the sink, plus a **water leak detector** (a $15 puck) on the cabinet floor.
- **Kick plate and toe kick:** the cabinet sits on the floor, and a wet mop line rots the toe kick. A silicone bead at the floor line prevents it.
- **A door that drags in winter** is a hinge loosening in swollen wood, or a house that has dried out and the frame moved. Check the hinge screws first: a stripped screw hole in a cabinet stile is fixed with a dowel and glue, or a longer screw, not with more turning.

**Use a damp cloth, not a wet one,** on painted cabinets. Water sitting on a painted cabinet edge wicks into the substrate.

## Caulk and grout: the two-week rule

- **Caulk at the tub and shower** is a wear item. Inspect it every six months for a gap at the wall or the tub line.
- **A hairline crack in grout** on a shower wall is a licence for water to sit in the wall. It should be raked out and regrouted, not caulked over.
- **Silicone at the floor transition** takes the most abuse in the house. If it is black or peeling, it is not sealing.
- **Grout is not waterproof;** it is water-resistant. The waterproofing behind the tile is what matters, and it is behind the tile.

## Mirrors, shelves, and the door swing

A bath is the room where a small fix most improves the space:

- **Mirror at eye level,** centred over the sink, and hung on a French cleat or two rated toggles, not a wire.
- **A medicine cabinet recessed** into a stud bay gives back the depth.
- **Door swing:** a bath door that opens in and hits the vanity is a pocket door or a swing reversal, which is a hinge-and-jamb job.
- **Exhaust grille cleaning:** a dust-clogged grille on a working fan cuts the flow hard. Vacuum it, and the fan does what it was rated for.

## The order to do it in

1. Measure the bath and pick a fan at 1 CFM per sq ft.
2. Confirm where the duct terminates. Outside, or it is not fixed.
3. Add a timer or humidity sensor so the fan runs past the shower.
4. Put a hygrometer in the room and watch it for a week.
5. Re-caulk the wet joints before they leak.
6. Fix the cabinet door and the toe kick before the box swells.
