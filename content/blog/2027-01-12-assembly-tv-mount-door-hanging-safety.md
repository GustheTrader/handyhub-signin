Everything in this post is the same skill at three scales: find the solid material behind the drywall, attach to it correctly, and know what you are allowed to drill into.

Most of the damage we repair comes from a drill going where a wire or a pipe was. The fix is a five-minute survey before the first hole.

## Find the structure before you find the anchor

- **Studs are 16 or 24 inches on centre.** A magnetic stud finder works by finding the drywall screws, which is more reliable than the electronic capacitance type on a textured wall.
- **Verify with a nail,** in a spot you will cover: a thin finishing nail through drywall into a stud stops dead. Into air, it does not.
- **Corners and beside openings** are almost always framing.
- **Above a window or door** there is a header, which is solid.
- **A stud is not a stud throughout:** a 2x4 wall has studs, but a cabinet anchor near a corner has a double or triple stud.

## Where the wires and pipes are, by rule of thumb

These are conventions, not guarantees. **A stud finder with AC detection is a $20 tool and this is why you own one.**

- **Receptacles and switches:** a wire runs vertically up or down from the box to the attic or the crawl space, and horizontally in the stud bay. Do not drill in the same bay directly above or below an outlet or switch.
- **A vertical line between a switch and a ceiling fixture** is a switch loop.
- **Plumbing:** wet walls back up to a bath, a kitchen, or a laundry. Inside a wet wall, supply lines run vertically and the drain is in the middle of a 2x4 or 2x6 wall. **Never drill into the middle 3 inches of a wet wall bay.**
- **A tub or shower wall** has a valve body at about 40 inches, with supplies going up or down from it.
- **Below a window on an exterior wall:** likely a hose bib supply, not always.
- **The ceiling below a bathroom** carries the drain from above, and drilling up into a ceiling to hang a fixture is how people find it.

**The safe band:** in a stud bay with no electrical or plumbing above or below it, the top 12 inches and the bottom of the bay are lower risk than the middle. That is not a permit to skip the scan.

## TV mounts: the load maths people skip

A 65-inch TV is 40–55 pounds. A full-motion mount adds 10–20 pounds of arm. Pulling the TV 18 inches off the wall multiplies the moment on the fasteners several times over.

- **Fixed and tilt mounts:** two studs is enough for a 55–75 inch TV, with four lag bolts.
- **Full-motion arms:** use both studs if they align with the plate, and a **1/2-inch or 5/8-inch plywood backer** if they do not. A 3/4-inch plywood backer spanning two studs behind the plate is the correct fix when the studs land wrong.
- **Drywall anchors alone are not rated for a full-motion arm** on a 65-inch TV, whatever the box says.
- **Lag bolts** should go 2 to 2.5 inches into the stud.
- **The mount centre** should be at eye level from the seating position, which is usually 42–48 inches to the centre of the screen, not a fixed height.

Two more things: check the **VESA pattern and weight rating** against the mount, and confirm there is a receptacle and a cable route where the TV will be. A mount installed over a missing outlet is a $300 electrical job after the fact.

## The wall-anchor actually rated for the job

| Load | Correct fastener |
| --- | --- |
| Picture, under 10 lb | Single nail in a hook, or a small self-drilling anchor |
| Shelf with books, 40 lb | Two toggle bolts into drywall, or into a stud |
| Cabinet, any weight | Into studs, always. A cabinet carries live load plus torque |
| Heavy mirror, 60 lb | Two 1/4-inch toggle bolts, or a French cleat into studs |
| Anything in a ceiling with vibration | Into joists |

**Toggle bolts beat every expansion anchor on drywall.** A rated drywall anchor holds in drywall, but drywall is paper over gypsum and it fails as a sheet, not at the fastener.

## Hanging a door so it closes the first time

A door that rubs the frame is a hinge or a jamb problem, not a door problem. The sequence:

1. **Plumb the frame.** A jamb that is out of plumb by 1/8 inch over the height will make any door bind. Shim the hinge side, then the strike side.
2. **Set the hinges with a 1/8-inch reveal** at the top and the hinge side. That is the visual standard.
3. **Top hinge first,** then shim the bottom to the reveal. Hang the door and check.
4. **Under-cut for flooring,** including the thickness of any transition strip. A door that clears bare floor and drags on carpet was undercut for the wrong condition.
5. **Strike plate last,** aligned with the latch as the door actually sits, not where it theoretically should.

**When a door rubs at the top corner opposite the hinge,** the hinge side is out of plumb. When it rubs at the strike side in the middle, the jamb is bowed inward.

Adjusting an existing door: loosen the top hinge screws and shim 1/16 inch behind the hinge leaf to throw the door away from the strike. Shimming the bottom hinge does the opposite.

## Assembly: the three-hour box of parts

Flat-pack furniture fails in two places: the fasteners and the floor.

- **Use the right driver bit.** A Phillips cam lock stripped is a cabinet that will not tighten again. Many brands use a Pozidriv; a Phillips bit will cam out and chew the head.
- **Do not fully tighten until it is square.** Assemble loose, square the diagonals with a tape measure (both diagonals equal means square), then torque.
- **Anchor tall units to the wall.** A tall bookshelf or dresser is a tip hazard, and the anti-tip strap is in the box for a reason.
- **Build on a blanket on a flat floor,** not on carpet, because carpet hides a racked frame until it is upright.

**Mounting and load-bearing exceptions:** anything that holds weight over a bed, a crib, or a seating area gets a stud or a rated toggle, not a plastic plug.

## The three-tool rule

Before any wall work: a **stud finder with AC detection**, a **short finishing nail**, and a **cordless driver with the right bit**. With those three, you will not put a lag bolt into a drain pipe, and the job will be square.
