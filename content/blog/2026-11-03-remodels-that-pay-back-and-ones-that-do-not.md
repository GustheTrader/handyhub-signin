Remodel decisions get made on emotion and defended on resale value. The two do not always point the same direction, and the numbers are looser than most contractors will tell you.

Two caveats before any of this applies to your house. The national cost-versus-value figures are a national average, and a mid-market or luxury finish in a neighborhood of 1990s tract homes does not recoup at the same rate. And a project done badly is not a remodel, it is a haircut.

## The two that mostly pay back

**Minor kitchen remodel**, meaning new cabinet faces or paint, new counter, new appliances, sink and hardware, without moving walls or plumbing. National recovery rates land in the range of **60 to 80 cents on the dollar**, and it is the remodels that move most reliably because the buyer's eye goes to the kitchen first.

**Bath remodel, mid-range**, with a new vanity, a re-tiled shower or tub surround, new fixtures, and improved ventilation. Reports put this around **55 to 70 percent**. The thing that quietly raises the value here is not the tile, it is the exhaust fan and the moisture control. A bath that stays dry does not grow a mold problem the buyer's inspector finds.

**Entry door replacement** is the consistent outlier. A new steel entry door reports the highest cost recovery of any common project, often in the **60 to 90 percent** range, because it is the cheapest exterior project that changes both the curb appeal and the security perception. This is the single best dollar-for-dollar project in our service list.

## The four that rarely pay back

**A kitchen luxury upgrade in a modest house.** If a full high-end kitchen costs $120,000 in a neighborhood whose ceiling is $550,000, the value added is not linear and the recovery rate falls hard. Do the kitchen, but finish it to the house, not to a magazine.

**A pool.** A pool in this climate is a maintenance liability for most buyers, not an amenity. The exception is a specific pocket where every comparable has one, and buyers expect it.

**A sunroom or addition that does not match.** A 12-by-16 addition that is a step down in finish from the main house reads as "there is an addition" to an appraiser and "we have to redo it" to a buyer.

**A garage conversion.** Turning a garage into a bedroom costs the house its covered parking, which is a line item every buyer in a snowy climate notices.

## What a buyer's inspector finds

The remodel advice nobody gives you: the projects you skip cost as much as the projects you pick, in the form of negotiation. The top defects that come back from inspections on Truckee Meadows homes are not aesthetics:

- **Missing bath exhaust or a fan vented into the attic** rather than outside. Cheap during a bath remodel, expensive after a mold finding.
- **Missing flashing at a door or window replacement.** A new door with a bad sill pan leaks and destroys the flooring in two winters. Recovery on the door is worth nothing if it leaks.
- **A dishwasher or ice maker supply line left unreplaced** during a kitchen remodel.
- **No expansion gap** under the new flooring against cabinets and door casings. This is the batch of calls we get in the first week of January.

A mid-range remodel that passes cleanly beats a high-end one with three findings, because findings cost you the sale or the margin.

## Getting a contractor in the Reno/Sparks market

Licensing below $1,000 of work in Nevada is thin, so a handyman or small contractor doing sub-$1,000 jobs may not hold the full license. That is acceptable for a repair and not for structural or plumbing or electrical work.

Ask every contractor for these **four things** before a deposit:

1. **Nevada license number** and a check that it is current on the state lookup.
2. **Certificate of insurance**, liability and workers' compensation, named to your address.
3. **A written scope** that names the squeamish parts: allowance amounts for tile and fixtures, and what happens if the price moves.
4. **A payment schedule with the final payment held until a walkthrough.** Never a large deposit up front, and never cash.
