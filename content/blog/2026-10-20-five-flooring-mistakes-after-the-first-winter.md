Most flooring install problems do not fail in the first month. They fail in January, when the furnace has been running for six weeks and the humidity indoors has dropped under 20%.

We get called for the same five things every season. Here is what each looks like, why it happens, and what it costs to correct.

## 1. Cupping at a cabinet or exterior wall

Cupping is when the board edges stand up and the center dips, so the floor feels like a washboard underfoot. It is almost always the edge of the floor taking on moisture from below or from the side.

The usual cause is a missing or short expansion gap. Wood moves across its width with the season, and every perimeter needs a gap. Under baseboard, the gap is then hidden by the trim. Where the installer ran the floor tight to a cabinet toe kick, a door casing, or a hearth, the board has nowhere to go and creeps upward instead.

**Fix:** in mild cases, a dehumidifier and a couple of weeks. In real cases, pull the baseboard, cut a proper gap with an oscillating tool, and re-set the trim. That is a $250–$600 repair for a moderate room, versus a full replacement if water is coming from below.

## 2. Gapping between planks at the seams

In dry heat, solid wood and some engineered products shrink. A 1/16-inch gap at the plank ends in October can open to 1/8 inch by January. That is normal movement, and a floor that was installed at 12% moisture content and then lives at 15% relative humidity will show it.

The mistake is choosing a product for this climate that cannot tolerate the swing. Solid 3/4-inch strip installed over a slab without a moisture test is the classic Truckee Meadows failure. A slab reading is the test that should have been done, and a product with a lower dimensional response is the answer.

**Fix:** if the gaps are consistent and under 1/8 inch, a humidifier set to 35% will close most of them. If they are localized to a wall or a doorway, the install is the problem and the floor needs to come up in that area.

## 3. Lifted or peaked seams

A ridge running the length of a seam is delamination in an engineered floor, or panel deflection over an uneven subfloor. Both trace back to prep.

The subfloor flatness spec most manufacturers publish is **3/16 inch in 10 feet**. That is not a suggestion. A centered hump or dip under a seam will telegraph through the floor within one heating season, because the HVAC dries the assembly and the deflection becomes a hard ridge.

**Fix:** grind or fill the substrate, and expect to lose a day. This is exactly the step that gets cut when a bid comes in low. Ask for the subfloor reading in writing.

## 4. Squeaks that appear where the floor meets the furnace

A squeak that starts in the first month of heat is usually not the floor. It is the **subfloor sheathing** moving against a joist, because the nails have loosened slightly and the dry air has shrunk the sheathing.

**Fix:** from below, if you have a basement or a crawl space, a shim and construction adhesive between the joist and the panel is a permanent fix for $100–$200 per spot. From above, finding the exact squeak location and driving a screw through the finished floor is cheap but cosmetic. The better answer is to confirm where the noise is with someone walking while you listen from below.

## 5. A kitchen floor that fails at the dishwasher and the ice box

The two places on any kitchen floor that fail first are under the dishwasher and at the fridge's ice maker line. Both are slow leaks that soak the subfloor from underneath without showing on the surface for months.

The install mistake is laying flooring and re-installing the appliances without replacing a braided supply line or checking the dishwasher pan. Reusing an old rubber hose when the box is already open is a $6 saving against a $1,400 floor.

**Fix:** replace both supply lines whenever an appliance comes out of a kitchen floor. This is one item worth insisting on.

## The free check to run before November

Before the heat comes on for good:

1. **Read the indoor humidity.** Anything under 30% for the season will move most wood floors.
2. **Walk every perimeter** with a flashlight angled low at the baseboard. Look for a board standing proud of the trim.
3. **Look under the sink cabinet** for staining at the toe kick, and at the fridge for a damp spot on the subfloor visible through the opening.
4. **Listen for squeaks** while the house is cold. A cold, dry house reveals the ones that are there.

If a floor is being replaced, ask for the subfloor flatness reading and the moisture reading on the slab in writing before the first plank goes down. Any installer who will not provide both is planning to cut that step.
