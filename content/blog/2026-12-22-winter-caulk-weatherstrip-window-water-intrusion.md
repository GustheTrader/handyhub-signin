The window that leaks in January is almost never a failed window. It is a bead of caulk that gave up last summer, and the water took the path of least resistance until it found your drywall.

Reno/Sparks makes this worse than most climates. Freeze-thaw cycles plus low humidity open and close exterior joints year round, and any gap that opens at 25 degrees on a January morning is a gap that water gets into when it rains in March.

## Why the leak shows up at the window when it started at the wall

Water follows framing. It runs down inside a wall cavity, hits a plate or a header, and then travels laterally until it finds a hole. The stain is where it exited, not where it entered.

**Four causes, in order of how often we find them:**

1. **Failed exterior caulk** at the window and door perimeter, especially the horizontal head joint. Water pools on a horizontal joint and that is where caulk ages first.
2. **Failed weatherstripping** on doors and operable windows. Not a water path, but it is an air path, and an air path in winter means a cold interior surface that condenses.
3. **Missing or failed backer rod** behind the caulk. Caulk cannot span a 1/2-inch gap by itself. Without a backer, it fails within two seasons.
4. **Weep holes blocked** in sliders and windows, which cause water to accumulate instead of drain.

## The inspection route, in order

Do it from the outside on a dry day. You are looking for gaps, not water on this pass.

1. **Head joint** of every window and door: the top horizontal. Run a fingertip along it. Any crack, any separation, any place the bead pulls away from the frame is a candidate.
2. **Sill and threshold:** look for the same, plus caulk that has gone chalky or is peeling. A sill that has dark staining where water sits is a leak that has been happening.
3. **Jamb verticals** at the bottom corners, where three planes meet. Corners fail before straight runs.
4. **Weep holes** at the bottom of sliders and any window with a track. They are 1/8-inch slots and they fill with debris over years.
5. **Interior drywall within 12 inches of the frame** under the opening. Soft drywall, bubbling paint, or a chalky white efflorescence line are all signs water has been there. A chalky line on drywall is salts from water that has dried repeatedly.
6. **Sill plates in the garage or basement** below the same wall. A wet sill plate under a window is a leak that has been happening for years.
7. **Weatherstripping** on every exterior door: compress it with a fingernail. It should spring back. Flat and brittle is dead.
8. **Door sweeps and thresholds:** light under the door, or a sweep that scrapes, means either an air path or a damaged seal.

Photograph everything as you go. A phone photo of the joint before you caulk is what turns a vague "leak somewhere" into a targeted repair.

## The right sealant, which is not silicone everywhere

This is where DIY weatherproofing fails most often.

| Location | Use | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Window and door perimeter, wood or fiber cement | **Elastomeric acrylic or siliconized acrylic**, paintable | Bare silicone, because it cannot be painted and bites poorly on painted surfaces |
| Metal frame to masonry or stucco | **100% silicone**, neutral cure | Latex caulk, which will not adhere to metal long term |
| Concrete sill or foundation joint | **Polyurethane sealant** | Acrylic, which cracks in freeze-thaw |
| Interior joints, trim to wall | **Paintable acrylic latex** | Silicone, for the same paintability reason |
| Any joint wider than 3/8 inch | **Backer rod plus sealant** | Sealant alone, always |

**Prep matters more than the product.** Caulk will not stick to dirt, to old silicone, or to a wet surface. Remove the old bead with a plastic scraper and a utility knife, clean with a degreaser, let it dry fully, then apply. A clean joint with mid-grade caulk outlasts a dirty joint with premium caulk every time.

**Tooling the bead:** a wet fingertip or a caulking tool, one pass, and do not go back over it. The joint should be slightly concave so water runs off rather than standing on it.

## Weatherstripping that actually stops the draft

- **V-strip (tension seal)** for the jamb of a door. It is the most durable option and it compresses rather than crushing.
- **Foam tape** is the cheap answer and lasts one to two seasons. Fine for a rental, not worth the labour in your own home.
- **Door sweep** for the bottom gap. A surface-mount sweep is a 20-minute install and it is the single biggest improvement in comfort for an exterior door.
- **Threshold replacement** when the gap is over 3/4 inch, because a sweep alone will not cover it.

An exterior door that passes the **dollar bill test** (close the door on a bill and pull) and lets it go is losing more heat than the window next to it.

## When it is not a caulk job

- **Water coming in above the window** on an upper floor with no caulk failure visible: suspect the siding, the flashing above the frame, or roof drainage. That is a different repair.
- **A window that leaks when the wind blows from one direction only** is usually a failed seal in the frame unit itself, and no caulk will fix it.
- **Mold or dark staining on interior drywall around the frame** means the leak has been active long enough to grow. Do not caulk over it; open it, dry it, and find the path.
- **A soft or spongy sill** on the interior side means the framing is compromised.
