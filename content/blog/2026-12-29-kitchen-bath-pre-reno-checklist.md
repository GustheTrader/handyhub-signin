A kitchen or bath remodel goes wrong in the first 48 hours, not at the finish. The failure is almost always something that was knowable before the crew arrived and was not written down.

This is the checklist we send before a start date. It is boring, and it saves weeks.

## 1. Shutoffs you can actually turn

Find the **angle stops** under the sink and behind the toilet. Turn each one closed and back open. A valve that will not move, or that weeps when it does, is a $15 part and a one-hour line item now, and an emergency on demo day.

Check the **main shutoff** too, and make sure everyone in the house knows where it is. A remodel that discovers a seized main valve on a Tuesday morning stops the job.

## 2. Water damage you did not know about

Bath remodels routinely uncover a rotted subfloor under the toilet, a failed shower pan liner that soaked the framing, or a slow toilet flange leak. None of these are surprises to an experienced contractor, and all of them are surprises to a homeowner when they hit day three.

**Open the walls where you can** or accept that the crew will. A good contract says how unforseen rot is priced, at what hourly rate, and that the work stops for a conversation rather than a surprise invoice.

## 3. Circuit capacity, before you buy the appliance

A 30-inch induction range needs a 40-amp, 240-volt circuit. A 30-inch gas range needs 120 volts and a gas line. A steam oven needs its own 20-amp. A bathroom with a heated floor needs a dedicated 240-volt circuit.

**The mistake:** buying the appliance and then discovering the panel has no room. Open the panel, count the empty slots, and photograph the breaker directory. If you are planning induction or a heated floor, talk to an electrician before the cabinets are ordered, because a panel upgrade is a $1,800–$3,500 decision that also moves the schedule.

## 4. Cabinet layout versus outlets

Under-cabinet lighting, a hood with a blower, a microwave, a disposal, and a dishwasher each need a receptacle or a circuit exactly where the cabinet will sit. Move a run of cabinets by 6 inches and every outlet is now inside a cabinet.

**Settle the appliance list and the cabinet layout in the same sitting.** The dimensional drawing from the cabinet manufacturer and the receptacle locations should be checked against each other before anything is ordered.

## 5. The boring one: who moves the stuff

A kitchen is three days of unload and one day of reload, and nobody plans for it. Empty the cabinets fully before the crew starts. A crew that has to move your dishes will move them to the floor of the room they are demolishing.

Plan for a **temporary kitchen**: a cleared counter somewhere else, a microwave, and a schedule for the sink outage. A kitchen gut takes 3–6 weeks and the sink is gone for most of it.

## 6. Ventilation

A bathroom **fan must exhaust outside,** not into the attic. In our climate, a fan that dumps into an attic adds moisture to a cold space, and that is how you get mold and attic frost. Confirm the existing fan duct route before tile goes up.

The **hood** needs the same check. A recirculating hood is not venting. A ducted hood needs a route and a wall cap, and a 900 CFM hood with a 4-inch duct is a 900 CFM hood that will be loud and useless.

## 7. Tile and slab lead times

Tile and countertop lead times are the single largest schedule risk in a bath or kitchen project.

- **Stock tile:** days.
- **Special order tile:** 2–4 weeks, and often one box short.
- **Quartz slab:** 1–3 weeks plus a templating appointment that requires the cabinets to be installed first.
- **Natural stone:** 2–6 weeks, and every slab varies.

Order the slab early, template after cabinets, and do not schedule the tile setter based on a delivery date that has not been confirmed in writing. Order 10% tile overage for cuts and mistakes.

## 8. Permits and inspections

Structural changes, new circuits, new plumbing runs, gas line changes, and water heater swaps all typically need a permit. A kitchen that moves a wall needs a structural review.

**Why this matters to you, not the contractor:** unpermitted work is a problem at resale, and insurance may decline a claim related to unpermitted electrical or plumbing. Get the permit number and keep the inspection records, not just the finished photos.

## 9. Access and dust

- **Where the crew parks,** and whether the dumpster sits on the street, in the driveway, or needs a permit.
- **How the work area gets sealed:** plastic sheeting and a zipper door from the rest of the house, because demolition dust gets into everything including your HVAC filter.
- **Whether the HVAC return is in the work zone.** Seal it if it is, or the whole house gets the dust.
- **Route to the bathroom** for the crew.

## 10. Materials on site, counted and photographed

Before the first day, photograph and count:

- Cabinets, by cabinet number against the manufacturer's order sheet
- Door and drawer fronts, hinges, and pulls
- Tile boxes, and the dye lot on every box (mismatched lots are visible on a floor)
- Plumbing rough-in materials
- Receptacles, switches, and plates

A missing cabinet box discovered on installation day is a 3-week delay.

## 11. A signed, itemized scope

The contract should name, line by line: what is included, what is excluded, the allowance for tile/lighting/fixtures, the change order process and hourly rate, the payment schedule tied to milestones, and who hauls the debris.

**The detail that costs the most money when it is missing is the allowance.** A $500 lighting allowance and a $3,000 fixture selection is a change order, and it is not a contractor's fault.

## 12. The damage deposit conversation

If you rent, get written permission. If you own, note in the contract who repairs drywall damage outside the work zone and who is responsible if a fixture arrives damaged.

## 13. Utilities and outages

Plan the water, power, and gas outage windows. A bathroom remodel takes the toilet out for a day and the shower out for days. Tell the household.

## 14. Insurance and licensing

Ask for the **license number** and verify it with the state, and ask for a **certificate of insurance** naming you as a certificate holder. A contractor without liability insurance who damages a water line is a contractor whose damage is your claim.

## 15. The one-week follow-up

Build the punch list walk into the contract before the final payment. Schedule it seven days after completion, once the caulk has cured and the paint has settled, and hold 10% until it is done.
