A ground fault circuit interrupter is the cheapest safety device in your house. It is also the one most often assumed to be working because the button clicks.

Here is what matters, in the order it matters.

## Where it is required

In a house built to modern code, GFCI protection is required in:

- Bathrooms, all receptacles
- Kitchens, countertop receptacles
- Garages and accessory buildings
- Outdoors, including decks and pool areas
- Laundry and utility areas with a sink
- Crawl spaces and unfinished basements
- Within six feet of any sink or wet bar

Nevada adopted the 2020 NEC as the basis for the current cycle, which also requires GFCI on **kitchen dishwashers and similar hardwired appliances**, not just the countertop outlets. If your panel has no GFCI breaker and the dishwasher is hardwired, that circuit is unprotected.

Older Reno homes often have one GFCI protecting a string of downstream outlets, and the protection is only as good as the last outlet in the chain. A downstream receptacle wired with the load and line reversed passes power but provides no protection. It looks fine and tests with a plug-in tester. It is not protecting anything.

## Why the test button is not a test

Pressing the button creates an internal imbalance and proves the trip mechanism and the contacts inside that device work. It does not prove:

- that the device is protecting anything downstream
- that the incoming wiring is correct
- that the device will trip at 5 milliamps in a real ground fault

For downstream protection and wiring, use a **plug-in GFCI tester with a test button at the farthest downstream outlet** on the circuit. Trip the upstream device from there. If the downstream outlet does not go dark, the chain is broken.

One thing worth knowing: a plug-in tester's test button is also not a complete proof, because it relies on the device's own sensing. It is still the best tool you have at home, and it catches the reversed load/line case, which is the common failure.

## The 3-light tester, and what the codes mean

A standard receptacle tester with three lamps tells you a lot in one plug:

| Reading | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Correct | Hot, neutral, ground wired right |
| Open ground | No ground path. Common in old two-wire homes |
| Open neutral | Neutral is disconnected somewhere |
| Open hot | No power on the hot leg |
| Hot/neutral reversed | Polarity swapped. Some electronics and lamps are damaged by this |

A three-light tester cannot detect a bootleg ground, where the ground terminal is jumped to neutral. That is the dangerous one, and it reads as correct. It is also why we pull the receptacle out and look at it on any home we are doing electrical work in.

## A tripping GFCI is usually right

The instinct is that a GFCI that keeps tripping is defective. Most of the time it is doing exactly its job and there is a real current leak:

- **A wet appliance.** A hair dryer or steam iron with moisture inside, especially after the first cold snap.
- **A worn-out device.** A bathroom outlet with thirty years of humid air is genuinely failing, internal leakage increasing until it trips.
- **A downstream fault.** A pinhole in a cable jacket where it passes through a stud, or an outdoor receptacle box that has filled with water.
- **A motor with a grounded winding.** A garbage disposal or pump with a failing winding trips a GFCI before it fails.

The specific case to worry about is a **GFCI tripping when a new appliance cycles on**. A refrigerator or a washer that trips the circuit at startup is drawing leakage to ground. That is a fault in the appliance, not a nuisance trip, and it should not be solved by removing the protection.

Replacing a tripping GFCI without finding the cause is how a real fault gets buried. If it trips again after a new device is installed, stop and call.

## The five-minute home audit

1. **Find every GFCI** in the house and list it. Bathrooms, kitchen, garage, exterior, laundry.
2. **At each one, press test, then reset.** If it does not reset into a firm click, replace it.
3. **Take a plug-in GFCI tester to the outlet farthest from each GFCI** and trip it from there. If the closest outlet stays live, the downstream wiring is wrong.
4. **Take a photo of the back of one receptacle** in any bathroom, if you feel comfortable with the breaker off. We are looking for a bootleg ground, reversed load/line, or aluminum pigtails.
5. **Check that exterior and garage receptacles still have a weather-resistant cover.** A cracked cover on a Tahoe-facing wall fills with snowmelt.

Call (555) 123-4567 to book an electrical safety visit. If a GFCI will not reset or keeps tripping after being swapped, that is the call that should not wait.

**This week's recommendation:** press test and reset on every GFCI in the house and time it. Any device that takes more than a second to reset is on its way out and costs less to replace now than after a failed inspection.
